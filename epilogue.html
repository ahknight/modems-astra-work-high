EPILOGUE  /  AFTER THE SPEAKER

The line stays open.

The calls gave way to connections that could stay up all day. Listen for the old engineering ideas in the systems that followed.

ISDN · A digital phone call

While voiceband modems were still climbing the speed ladder, homes with Basic Rate ISDN could already dial a provider digitally. Two 64 kbit/s bearer channels carried voice or data, and a separate 16 kbit/s channel handled call signaling. You could keep one bearer for the telephone or combine both for a nominal 128 kbit/s before overhead. The familiar Point-to-Point Protocol (PPP) survived the change of line. Its Multilink extension let the far end reassemble a packet sent across two calls.

DSL · More room on the same pair

The twisted pair into the house could carry frequencies that the switched voice service discarded. ADSL put a data transceiver at each end of that local pair, allowing voice and broadband to coexist. Discrete multitone modulation split the available spectrum into many narrow carriers. Training measured each one. Clean carriers took more bits; noisy ones took fewer. If interference changed, the modems could move bits between carriers during the connection. John Cioffi and colleagues helped develop this approach, standardized internationally in G.992.1 in 1999.

Sources

DOCSIS · A neighborhood shares the cable

The first DOCSIS specification arrived in 1997 through the MCNS cable-operator consortium, CableLabs and industry partners. It let a cable modem and a headend from different vendors speak the same language over a shared cable plant. The neighborhood now had to share upstream airtime. Ranging helped bursts arrive at the headend on time and at a usable level. Later DOCSIS 3.1 spread downstream data across many tightly spaced carriers. The speaker had disappeared, but the modem was still measuring distortion and learning how to be heard.
