The line keeps the same beat. The receiver learns to distinguish four times as many symbols.

THE BREAKTHROUGH  /  1984

Imagine keeping time to the same 600 ticks per second. V.22 gave a wave four possible turns. Its 1984 successor, CCITT V.22bis, also varies the wave’s size. Two bits select a change of quadrant relative to the last one. Two more select one of four positions within the new quadrant. Each symbol now carries four bits, giving a nominal 2,400 bit/s in the same two frequency bands.

Engineers draw these positions as dots called a constellation. Moving outward means increasing the wave’s amplitude. Moving around the centre means changing its phase. Sixteen possible landing places give the receiver a finer distinction to make. Noise or distortion that would have left the old quadrant obvious can put a point closer to the wrong neighbour.

A V.22bis pair begins in the 1,200-bit/s language both generations understand. The caller sends a short alternating pattern of the bit pairs 00 and 11. A compatible answerer sends it back. After training at 1,200 bit/s both switch to the denser signal and look for known bits before releasing user data. An older V.22 partner can complete a 1,200-bit/s call without answering that invitation.

The 1,200-bit/s fallback is built into the design. A separate optional procedure in the 1988 edition lets modems request a speed change during an established call, preserving the telephone connection. A noisy evening need not mean dialing the number all over again.

FIELD NOTE

One word in the standard

Bis marks another version in this family of recommendations. V.22bis keeps enough of V.22 to strike up a conversation with an older machine. The short 00/11 exchange is where two newer machines recognize each other.

Listening transcript

Select a stage to move the playhead. Every description stays here as the signal plays.

  1. The answering modem sends repeated pairs of ones, each turning the carrier back by a quarter turn. This produces the steady high whistle that the caller recognizes.

  2. The caller alternates two particular pairs of bits, 00 and 11, for a tenth of a second. This short signature says it can use the larger alphabet.

  3. The answer modem returns the same signature in its own band. The caller has already switched to scrambled ones at 1,200 bit/s.

  4. Both ends send scrambled ones. Each listens to the partner’s known pattern and prepares to distinguish points closer together.

  5. The answer modem switches to four bits per symbol. The caller follows about one tenth of a second later. The clock still ticks 600 times a second.

  6. The high band now carries an actual framed message, “CARRIER ARCHIVE ONLINE” followed by “LOGIN: ”. User data sounds much like scrambled training. The wire has no special connected chime.

  7. The caller sends “HELLO FROM HOME”. Both transmitters continue sending scrambled idle ones after the characters run out.

How this signal was made

Transmitter reconstruction of the V.22bis constant-carrier startup, beginning at the answer mark. The international V.25 preamble is omitted, as permitted on some national connections. The model follows nominal timing, differential quadrant changes, the 14/17-bit-tap scrambler and 75% roll-off pulse shaping at 600 baud. The guard tone is disabled. It assumes a clean line, no propagation delay and successful detection. Real equalizers, carrier offsets, speaker response and national compromise filters are outside the model. The 8N1 terminal messages and typing pauses are illustrative.
