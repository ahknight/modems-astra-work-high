THE BREAKTHROUGH  /  1984

At 2,400 bit/s, each direction had its own slice of the voice band. V.32 put both transmitters on the same nominal 1,800 Hz carrier and raised the pace from 600 to 2,400 symbols a second. Now the receiver could hear a loud copy of its own transmission mixed with the quieter reply. It knew what it had sent. During training it learned how the circuit delayed and distorted that signal, so it could subtract a prediction of the echo. 2

Listen for the two questions in the opening exchange. The caller holds one pitch. The answering modem returns a low and a high pitch together. Each can flip its wave halfway around and time the other modem’s response. The sharper, less musical training that follows gives the receivers a known pattern against which to adjust. An equalizer compensates for the way one symbol can smear into its neighbour. 2

The CCITT approved V.32 in 1984. Its 1988 edition specifies a 16-point mode for 9,600 interoperability and an optional 32-point trellis-coded alternative. Both deliver four information bits per symbol. In the coded version, the preceding choices restrict which point comes next. A receiver can weigh a whole succession of points when a single noisy one looks ambiguous. IBM researcher Gottfried Ungerboeck pioneered this combination of coding and modulation. 1234

A bulletin-board operator had another option. The original U.S. Robotics Courier HST gave a file transfer 9,600 bit/s in one direction and left 300 bit/s for replies. The fast direction could switch when the traffic did. Two boxes marked “9600” could therefore be promising quite different conversations. 6

FIELD NOTE

Which 9,600?

G. David Forney Jr. and the Codex team had already built high-speed modems for private lines. Their 9600C fed into V.29 for four-wire leased circuits. The milestone here is simultaneous two-way conversation over an ordinary dialled two-wire call. The ITU’s approval record dates V.32 to 1984, although some retrospective histories say 1986.

Listening transcript

Select a stage to move the playhead. Every description stays here as the signal plays.

  1. The answering modem sends 2,100 Hz with half-turn phase reversals, a request to disable network echo cancellation.

  2. The caller adds a steady 1,800 Hz. Its tone will overlap the answering modem’s signals.

  3. The answerer sends 600 and 3,000 Hz together. The ends take turns reversing phase, timing the replies to estimate delay.

  4. A regular S pattern and its brief reversal set the timing. The signal then opens into a reproducible stream of scrambled ones for echo and equalizer training.

  5. The answering modem repeats a 16-bit R1 word offering 4,800 and 9,600 bit/s and trellis coding.

  6. The caller supplies its own known sequence. Each direction uses a different scrambler so the two signals can be distinguished.

  7. The caller offers the simpler 16-point mode. The answerer trains again, selects 9,600, and both ends mark agreement with an E word.

  8. The caller switches to four information bits per symbol and sends scrambled ones. The answerer follows. The receivers open their data outputs after the final preamble.

  9. The answerer now encodes the text “Welcome to the signal archive!” The caller sends “LIST”. Both messages repeat. The equal-looking fuzz now carries recoverable bytes in both directions.

How this signal was made

Synthesis begins at V.25 answer tone, after dialing and the initial off-hook silence. The caller answers after 1.02 s. V.25 permits early termination after detecting its response for 100 ms, so the 1.2 s tone is a permitted example. One-way delay is 10 ms. Training lengths and finite root-raised-cosine transmit filters are example choices. No channel or receiver is simulated. Includes reciprocal training, R1/R2/R3, E, scrambled B1 and synchronous 16-QAM data. The chosen payload repeats plain ASCII bytes least-significant bit first, with no link-layer framing.
