THE BREAKTHROUGH  /  1970s

Lee Felsenstein remembered the terminal at Leopold’s dialing San Francisco once a day and staying on the line. That first installation ran at 110 baud. His Pennywhistle design grew out of the Resource One project’s need for affordable terminal links in 1973. By March 1976, readers of Popular Electronics could build their own 300-bit/s modem for under $100. The prototype and the magazine kit account for two dates often attached to the same design.

The Pennywhistle’s receiver used the steady tone between characters as a reference. That let it follow a drifting signal, even from a cassette running a little off speed. Dennis Hayes and circuit designer Dale Heatherington took a different route into the home. By August 1977, BYTE was describing their 80-103A, an S-100 board whose host computer could dial or answer. An unattended computer could now pick up when somebody called.

Both ends had to agree on the tones. In the Bell 103A2 arrangement a caller sends a one at 1,270 Hz and a zero at 1,070 Hz. The answering station uses 2,225 and 2,025 Hz respectively. Each change of pitch can convey one bit. With one start bit, eight data bits and one stop bit, 300 bit/s buys 30 characters each second. Leave the keyboard alone and the idle mark can whistle continuously.

A second 300-bit/s family, CCITT V.21, used a different set of pitches. Owning a “300 baud” modem told you the speed. Knowing whether the far end spoke Bell 103 or V.21 told you whether the two could converse.

FIELD NOTE

A modem you could build

“it turned out because it was self-adjusting, it could be made as a kit.” — Lee Felsenstein, interviewed by Kip Crosby for the Computer History Museum, 2008, p. 18. His circuit spared the builder a delicate receiver adjustment.

Listening transcript

Select a stage to move the playhead. Every description stays here as the signal plays.

  1. The distant modem holds its high mark at 2,225 Hz. This excerpt starts at the first sound, after the telephone exchange has answered.

  2. The caller adds a steady 1,270 Hz. Two pitches travel together, one in each direction. Both ends now have a carrier to detect.

  3. The answering terminal sends “CARRIER ARCHIVE ONLINE” and a login prompt. Every zero pulls the high pitch down to 2,025 Hz. The caller still holds a steady mark.

  4. The user sends “HELLO FROM HOME”. The lower voice now switches between 1,070 and 1,270 Hz. After the message both ends hold mark, ready for another character.

How this signal was made

Reconstruction of a Bell 103A2 channel establishment, starting with the answer mark. The caller is already in data mode. The nominal 150 ms carrier-detection interval and additional 1.5 s wait come from Bell’s interface sequence. Payloads and typing delays are illustrative. Both messages are encoded at 300 bit/s, with one start bit, eight data bits and one stop bit. This is an ideal electrical signal with no acoustic-cup or line distortion.
