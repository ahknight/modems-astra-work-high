The exchange becomes the transmitter.

THE BREAKTHROUGH  /  1998

Imagine dialing a friend who bought the very same 56K modem. You could connect, but both of you plugged into analogue phone jacks. For the fast direction, one end needed digital access to the telephone network. An internet provider could buy that access and send the exchange the actual numbers that its voice decoder would turn into voltages. 15

The network accepted 8,000 pulse-code modulation (PCM) samples each second. Your modem learned which output levels it could distinguish after their journey down your line. The provider then chose among those levels to carry the download. At the V.90 ceiling this averaged seven information bits per sample. On upload, the exchange still had to measure an incoming analogue waveform and round it to a voice code. V.90 used V.34 signaling in that direction, up to 33,600 bit/s. 1

V.8 opened the call by exchanging capabilities, including the type of network access at each end. V.8 bis was optional. Next the modems probed the line and measured the return delay. Training compensated for echoes and smearing; the home modem could request particular PCM levels to discover what the network did to them. In the final phase the pair settled the settings, sent the last known training symbols, and switched to user data. Line noise, extra conversions and transmit-power limits could all lower the negotiated rate. U.S. Robotics warned North American customers that power limits could cap downloads near 53.3 kbit/s. 15

Buying a 56K modem in 1997 meant checking what your provider owned. U.S. Robotics sold x2, while Rockwell and Lucent backed the incompatible K56flex. Engineer Brent Townshend licensed his approach to U.S. Robotics. ITU-T Study Group 16 brought manufacturers to a common specification. Reports in February 1998 describe the agreement on V.90; the ITU records formal approval on September 25. Those dates mark different events. 234

V.92, approved in 2000, added PCM upload up to 48 kbit/s on suitable connections, quicker reconnection over a recognized path, and a way to hold a session while answering call waiting. V.44 was a separate compression agreement based on work by Hughes Network Systems. It could shorten a transfer when the bytes contained repeated patterns. It could not change the line rate. 67

Listening transcript

Select a stage to move the playhead. Every description stays here as the signal plays.

  1. After a short silence the answering modem sends 2,100 Hz. Its strength ripples 15 times per second. Phase reversals ask network echo cancellers to stand aside.

  2. Listen to the low caller and higher reply. At 300 bit/s they advertise data service, V.34 fallback and complementary V.90 roles. Three framed zero bytes acknowledge the agreement.

  3. Each end sends the same 21 pitches, first loud and then 6 dB quieter. The far end compares what it receives. This edit moves directly between the two probe bursts.

  4. A 48 ms repeating pattern briefly reverses, then opens into a steady hiss. A digital scrambler determines every sign in that hiss. The receiver knows what to expect and can correct the line’s smearing.

  5. Which levels survive the route? This example alternates a known reference with test levels of increasing magnitude. The recurring swells are the same 64-level scan repeated six times.

  6. After final negotiation, 48 frames of known bits lead straight into a repeated text message, “CARRIER IS UP. HELLO FROM THE DIGITAL END.” Six samples now carry 42 bits. The remote speaker would usually be silent by this point.

How this signal was made

Edited synthesis at native speed. V.8 menus, line probes, Sd/TRN1d, a chosen digital-impairment pattern, and B1d into 56,000 bit/s payload use documented signal rules. The timeline cuts out INFO/ranging, most upstream training and the final parameter exchange. The last two sections use example parameters, including an ideal 64-level magnitude set and no spectral shaping. PCM is decoded with North American μ-law. Listen levels are scaled for playback. No line distortion, telephone speaker, or recording is modeled.
