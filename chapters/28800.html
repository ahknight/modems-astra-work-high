THE BREAKTHROUGH  /  1994

V.32bis had a fixed symbol clock. In 1994 the ITU-T approved V.34, developed under the working name V.fast. It could change the clock, carrier and amount of high-frequency emphasis to suit the call. A better circuit could carry a broader signal. The required symbol rates were 2,400, 3,000 and 3,200 per second, with three additional choices optional. 12

After the V.8 exchange identifies a mutually supported modem mode, V.34 sends a short chord of 21 pitches. Four positions in its evenly spaced pattern are deliberately empty. The receiver knows what went out, so it can measure which notes arrived weak and whether distortion spilled energy into the empty slots. The first burst, L1, is 6 dB louder than the following L2. Later, the modems train their equalizers and echo cancellers, then exchange the settings for data. 2

Getting twice the previous top rate took more than a wider signal. V.34 grouped symbols when coding and shaping them. Shell mapping favoured groups needing less average transmit power, leaving more room between distinguishable choices for a given power budget. Motorola’s Vedat Eyuboglu helped develop the standard. The Motorola engineers’ contemporary account connects the mathematics to measurements on actual telephone circuits. 3

FIELD NOTE

One clock, nine bits on average

A 28,800 bit/s call at 3,200 symbols/s delivers nine information bits per symbol on average. Other valid clock and data-rate combinations need fractional averages. V.34 spreads the bits across groups of symbols to achieve them.

Listening transcript

Select a stage to move the playhead. Every description stays here as the signal plays.

  1. At 2,100 Hz the answer tone swells and shrinks 15 times a second. This V.8 variant also reverses phase at 450 ms intervals.

  2. The caller sends its menu in the low V.21 band. The answerer repeats their common modes in the high band. Three framed zero bytes close the exchange.

  3. Each end sends a checked INFO0 message with its clock and carrier options. These brief words ride on different carriers.

  4. The answering modem plays a 21-note chord for 160 ms, then the same chord 6 dB quieter. The four empty frequencies help reveal distortion.

  5. The calling modem sends the same two-level probe. The two ends can now compare estimates for each direction.

  6. The example reports a clean path. The receiver’s results lead to a 3,200-symbol/s clock and 1,920 Hz carrier in both directions.

  7. An alternating pair marks the start of training. Six turns through a known 48-symbol pattern give the receiver a reference. Scrambled ones follow it.

  8. The caller sends S, the periodic PP sequence and four-point training on the selected carrier. Both modems learn their own echo while the far receiver learns its equalizer.

  9. The answering modem marks the move to final training. Both directions now train at once using separate scrambled sequences.

  10. The MP messages specify a maximum of 28,800 in each direction. Their acknowledgments and end markers conclude this excerpt.

How this signal was made

Synthesized V.8 menus, V.34 INFO messages with CRC, phase-reversal ranging, L1/L2, S, PP, four-point TRN, J, MP acknowledgments and E. This fixed clean-line scenario selects symmetric rates, zero frequency offset and no vendor-specific training. Pulse shaping, levels, receiver timing and training lengths are illustrative. Stops before the trellis-coded B1 preamble and payload. No recording or added noise.
