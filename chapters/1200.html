THE BREAKTHROUGH  /  1979–80

At 300 bit/s a receiver asks which pitch it hears. The international CCITT V.22 design gives each direction its own carrier, nominally 1,200 Hz for the caller and 2,400 Hz for the answerer. Six hundred times a second, each receiver compares the new wave with the one just before it. The change in its position within a cycle is called a phase change. 1

Picture the wave as a hand going round a clock. At each symbol boundary the transmitter may leave that hand on course, move it a quarter turn forwards or backwards, or move it half a turn. The four choices stand for the four possible pairs of bits. Twice the symbol rate of the 300-bit/s link and twice the bits per symbol give 1,200 bit/s in each direction. 1

Startup gives the receiver familiar material before it has to read a stranger’s message. In the V.22 constant-carrier sequence, the answerer first sends a repeating, unscrambled stream of ones. The caller replies with ones passed through a scrambler, and the answerer switches to the same kind of signal. A scrambler reversibly stirs the bits into a changing pattern so even a steady input exercises the receiver’s timing. 1

The international design and Bell’s 212A are distinct 1,200-bit/s systems. For example, the Bell setup uses a 2,225 Hz answering tone. V.22 also allowed an optional 600-bit/s mode for one bit per symbol and national choices of guard tone. The ITU catalogue lists V.22 editions in 1979 and 1980. The detailed signal description here follows its 1988 revision. 123

FIELD NOTE

Baud counts decisions

If the receiver decides between four alternatives 600 times a second, it receives 1,200 bits/s at 600 baud. Carrier frequency tells you how fast the underlying wave cycles. Symbol rate tells you how often it can say something new.

Listening transcript

Select a stage to move the playhead. Every description stays here as the signal plays.

  1. The answering modem sends repeated pairs of ones, each turning the carrier back by a quarter turn. This produces the steady high whistle that the caller recognizes.

  2. The low band fills with scrambled ones. Although the underlying bit is always one, the scrambler supplies changing bit pairs so the receiver can find the symbol clock.

  3. The high band changes from a whistle to a rough texture as the answering modem also sends scrambled ones. Both voices now make four possible phase steps.

  4. The high band now carries an actual framed message, “CARRIER ARCHIVE ONLINE” followed by “LOGIN: ”. User data sounds much like scrambled training. The wire has no special connected chime.

  5. The caller sends “HELLO FROM HOME”. Both transmitters continue sending scrambled idle ones after the characters run out.

How this signal was made

Transmitter reconstruction of the V.22 constant-carrier startup, beginning at the answer mark. The international V.25 preamble is omitted, as permitted on some national connections. The model follows nominal timing, differential quadrant changes, the 14/17-bit-tap scrambler and 75% roll-off pulse shaping at 600 baud. The guard tone is disabled. It assumes a clean line, no propagation delay and successful detection. Real equalizers, carrier offsets, speaker response and national compromise filters are outside the model. The 8N1 terminal messages and typing pauses are illustrative.
