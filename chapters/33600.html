THE BREAKTHROUGH  /  1996

The ITU-T’s October 1996 revision kept the name V.34. Study Group 14 reported the new ceiling at the Geneva standardization conference. At Motorola, Les Brown represented the company on modem standards committees and Jack Moran surveyed real telephone paths to build more representative tests. The distinction mattered. A design that worked over one pristine connection still had to recognize what a poor one would bear.

The revised recommendation added two optional rates, 31,200 and 33,600 bit/s. Top rate needs the fastest V.34 clock, about 3,429 symbols/s. At that clock, the standard offers 512 points with minimum shaping for 28,800, and 1,408 for 33,600. Expanded shaping allows 640 and 1,664 respectively. More possible points in the same power budget leave smaller margins for noise. Both ends need the option and a sufficiently clean path.

The probe itself barely gives the upgrade away. In final training the modems send an MP message containing their supported rates and limits for each direction. One capability bit advertises 33,600. A lower rate can win, and asymmetry is possible when both sides enable it. The old opening sound can therefore precede several different connect messages.

FIELD NOTE

What 56K would change

V.34 was solving the problem of two analogue modems at the ends of a voice call. A provider connected digitally to the phone network could change one end of that problem. In December 1996, Forney and colleagues already reported that manufacturers were planning rates above 33.6 kb/s.

Listening transcript

Select a stage to move the playhead. Every description stays here as the signal plays.

  1. At 2,100 Hz the answer tone swells and shrinks 15 times a second. This V.8 variant also reverses phase at 450 ms intervals.

  2. The caller sends its menu in the low V.21 band. The answerer repeats their common modes in the high band. Three framed zero bytes close the exchange.

  3. INFO0 identifies the optional fast clock and the larger signal alphabet needed at 33,600. A dedicated bit advertises the larger constellation.

  4. The answering modem plays a 21-note chord for 160 ms, then the same chord 6 dB quieter. The four empty frequencies help reveal distortion.

  5. The calling modem sends the same two-level probe. The two ends can now compare estimates for each direction.

  6. The example reports a clean path. INFO1 selects about 3,429 symbols per second and a carrier near 1,959 Hz for both ends.

  7. An alternating pair marks the start of training. Six turns through a known 48-symbol pattern give the receiver a reference. Scrambled ones follow it.

  8. The caller sends S, the periodic PP sequence and four-point training on the selected carrier. Both modems learn their own echo while the far receiver learns its equalizer.

  9. The answering modem marks the move to final training. Both directions now train at once using separate scrambled sequences.

  10. Both MP messages enable 33,600 and request it in both directions. Their acknowledgments and end markers conclude this excerpt.

How this signal was made

Synthesized V.8 menus, V.34 INFO messages with CRC, phase-reversal ranging, L1/L2, S, PP, four-point TRN, J, MP acknowledgments and E. This fixed clean-line scenario selects symmetric rates, zero frequency offset and no vendor-specific training. Pulse shaping, levels, receiver timing and training lengths are illustrative. Stops before the trellis-coded B1 preamble and payload. No recording or added noise.
